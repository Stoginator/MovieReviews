Tanish's Movie Reviews

The Dark Knight

September 5, 2026 - 10:05pm - Standard

What do you believe in?

Do I really look like a guy with a plan?

Spoilers Ahead

The Dark Knight begins over logos, with the sound of a bat's wings reverberating. The eerie soundstage gives way to an image of blue flame. The Batman's logo pushes towards us, like the character's version of the James Bond gun-barrel. This time, Batman's theme doesn't play; rather, a synthesizer fades in.

BOOM!

A crystal-clear, screen-filling aerial view of downtown Gotham (née Chicago) appears. The sounds of a city – cars moving, honking, distant sirens – are overpowered by the creepy synthesizer. It doesn't relent until –

POP!

A window shatters. High above the streets, two clowns are up to something, as we hear the gritty sounds of loading a metal arrow launcher. A clown fires it.

CUT TO:

At street-level, the synthesizer returns for a second time, ascending and ascending. The camera pushes in behind a man holding a clown mask. A metro passes in the distance, but no sound survives the synth – until a car screeches to a halt. The man puts on the mask and gets in. The game is on.

As the bank heist begins, the camera directs our eyes towards this clown. He's quiet and observant. There's something different about him.

One by one, the other clowns complete their assignments in pairs: Disabling the alarm, arming civilians with live grenades, and unlocking a vault. However, their final task is to kill their partners – a part of the job that they're happy to complete.

As they each fall, only one remains: The mastermind of this successful and sadistic plot. The synthesizer returns and he unmasks himself. The Joker has arrived.

His musical theme overtaking the city's ambience foreshadows how oppressive his criminal regime will become, even to the Batman. The forceful sound of bat wings cannot undo a high-pitched synthesizer.

  • THE JOKER
  • This is what happens when an unstoppable force meets an immovable object.

The people of Gotham City can't catch a break. First, it was the mob bosses, who dealt in drugs and bribery. For all the lawlessness they introduced, they had boundaries they would not cross: Never target the families of your enemies. Under their rule, Gotham became a hopeless city.

Then came the Batman (Christian Bale): A figure who used military-grade tools against criminality. He was an unaccountable vigilante, who operated with one rule: Don't kill. After years of mob-induced fear, he was a force from outside the justice system trying to restore civilization. The incorruptible symbol of Batman brought back hope.

Now comes the Joker: The first, great counter-reaction to the Batman. As a terrorist, he uses military-grade tools to bring the city to its knees, willing to kill anyone in his way. Unlike the mob bosses, the Joker has no principles: The families of enemies are fair game. He is Batman's equivalent, on the other side of justice.

With the Joker at large, Gotham must confront a hard truth: Without the Batman, there would be no Joker. All that separates the hero and the villain are rules. Their duel of wits and carnage is the worst-case scenario of transferring power to unaccountable men.

For the Batman, the Joker's philosophy cuts deeper into his symbol than any mob boss. What is the proper response to an enemy who is an existential threat? To defeat such an enemy, should you change your principles?

Beneath face paint, Heath Ledger is unrecognizable as the Joker. Usually, his charisma bonds us to a hero; here, he draws us into the mind of a sadist. With Ledger, writer-director Christopher Nolan cast against type so the audience could understand a villain – and not immediately reject him for his inhumanity.

The Joker drives the film despite being onscreen for a fifth of it. His schemes demand such extraordinary measures that when things start to go bad, it feels like there's no floor. His evil is uncomfortable and all-enveloping. Ledger's performance, which is the stuff of legend, enables the character to leap into our psyche. He's there even when he's not.

The Joker's playground is Gotham. Aerial, IMAX-sized views establish the urban sprawl. Scenes take place on all levels, from street parades and tunnels to Bruce's penthouse and a top-floor boardroom. In the mayor's office, the city presses through the glass. When the Joker takes over the news, he's not a comic-book villain anymore; he's a terrorist holding an American city hostage.

❮
❯

Aside from Batman, the city has only one legitimate, saving grace: Harvey Dent (Aaron Eckhart), the political poster-child of renewal. For a city desperate for the rule of law, he is a dream come true. A fresh face, symbolizing fearlessness and optimism. He's one of the bulwarks that the Joker will have to bring down.

Like Batman Begins before it, The Dark Knight runs heavy on philosophy, but not in an academic way. It's dramatized through action. The Joker believes the natural order is disorder. The things that men have created – government, hospitals, roads, cities – are the objects he wants to disrupt and burn. He believes that civilization hides our cruel and indecent nature. He wants you to think he's shallow, but he isn't.

The Joker shares two stories from his personal life, where members of his family are mutilated by knives. In one story, he harms himself with a knife to prove he doesn't care about his wife's facial scars.

Whether they are true or not, they feed his immutable worldview. His use of violence puts him "ahead of the curve." He wants to prove that, given the right circumstances, others will join him. He places his victims in horrible situations, where he can pitch them on his extremist beliefs. When he begins to speak, watch out. You don't know who he will convince.

The Joker pitches three audiences on his philosophy: Gotham, Batman, and Harvey Dent. On Gotham Cable News, he blackmails the city:

  • THE JOKER
  • Batman must take off his mask, and turn himself in. Oh and every day he doesn't, people will die. Starting tonight. I'm a man of my word.

By creating a pressure cooker in the city, he causes unrest and lawlessness as citizens turn on each other. At the whims of a terrorist, they are faced with impossible decisions: Do I kill a man to save my wife in the hospital?

With his plan wreaking havoc in the streets, the Joker feels emboldened enough to pitch the Batman. In the famous interrogation scene between them, the villain wants to undermine Batman's philosophy. And, if words won't convince him, his plan will force him to break his one rule.

The Joker's play is to convince Batman that they are more alike than different. Here, he offers the clearest indication of his true goal:

  • THE JOKER
  • You'll see. I'll show you... when the chips are down, these civilized people... they'll eat each other.

But Batman is driven into fury by this worldview. He levels the Joker with punches, but each one deals the opposite effect. The Joker enjoys the beating, laughing hysterically.

The Joker's pitch fails on the Batman, but he takes rejection very well. He has strategically set up his next pitch for Harvey Dent at his most vulnerable. Having killed Rachel, Dent's blushing bride-to-be, and burned half of Dent's face, the Joker tries to prove that the events weren't his fault.

Dent initially resists, but the hospital has strapped him to the bed. He can't help but listen. The wheels turn in Dent's head. He wants someone to explain his trauma. No one in the city could contain the Joker. They all failed him, especially the Batman.

  • THE JOKER
  • I'm an agent of chaos. And you know the thing about chaos, Harvey? It's fair.

With that, whatever faith Dent had in the system is gone. He realizes that the structure of a civilized world is not enough to contain uncivilized men, like the Joker. Dent's pain leads him to repudiate what he was a paragon of: the justice system. He is convinced that the system's frailty is why Rachel died.

  • DENT
  • The Joker's just a mad dog. I want whoever let him off the leash.

Dent becomes a vigilante to deal vengeance in a way the justice system never could. He flips a coin to determine whether to execute his enemies, like a mob boss or the cops who betrayed him.

The Joker's pitch succeeds: Gotham's symbol of moral renewal becomes a symbol of its rot. The arc of The Dark Knight runs through Harvey Dent.

To find the Joker, Batman puts together a system of mass surveillance, listening in on every cell phone in the city. The CEO of Wayne Enterprises, Lucius Fox (Morgan Freeman), cannot believe his eyes:

  • FOX
  • Beautiful. Unethical. Dangerous. You've turned every phone in the city into a microphone...
  • BATMAN
  • I've got to find this man, Lucius.
  • FOX
  • But at what cost?

Batman's one rule is simple, but a situation like this exposes its limits. One rule is not an ethics. It cannot answer whether Batman should invade the privacy of millions of citizens to locate a terrorist. Batman holds the power of mass surveillance, acting as a form of one-man rule over Gotham. His decisions have cost lives. So far, we've agreed with him. What happens if Batman cracks like Harvey Dent?

The film's final confrontation between Batman, Dent, and Jim Gordon (Gary Oldman) finds Dent with a gun on Gordon's son, ready to pull the trigger based on the flip of a coin. He wants Gordon to know what the loss of Rachel felt like.

To save Gordon's son, Batman takes them both over a ledge. Dent falls several stories, crystallizing his fall from grace, but the boy is saved. Lacking the strength to pull himself up, Batman falls the same way.

In the rubble, the two fallen symbols of Gotham lie together: one masked in matte black armor and the other maskless in a burnt suit. Only one survived the Joker's test, keeping his principles intact.

Gordon helps Batman up. We see Batman crouch again to turn Dent's head to his good side. The gears turn in Batman's head, until he says:

  • BATMAN
  • I killed those people. That's what I can be.

To save Gotham City, Batman will assume responsibility for Dent's actions. He justifies the lie:

  • BATMAN
  • Sometimes, the truth isn't good enough. Sometimes people deserve more. Sometimes, people deserve to have their faith rewarded.

In a few sentences, Batman explains the promise and the peril of political movements – both real and fictional. Harvey Dent was supposed to be Gotham's savior: "I believe in Harvey Dent" was the people's slogan. If the truth of his demise became known, their optimism might never return. To spare them, Batman constructs a lie.

Batman's decision is a Pyrrhic victory. To preserve what Dent meant to Gotham, he sacrifices his own symbol and exiles himself. He makes the democratic choice, returning his overwhelming power to the people and their accountable political institutions. The Batman will no longer be there to save the day, even though Gotham still needs him.

Yet, the lie may be his most undemocratic action: He cannot entrust his city with the truth. He has seen the Joker's final pitch fail. Two ferries, one of criminals and one of civilians, each holding the detonator to the other. Thirty minutes go by. Nothing happens. Gotham refuses to bow to a terrorist's threats. Despite this, Batman doesn't trust the city.

With the decision made, it's time for Batman to leave. However, his exit isn't a disappearance as usual. This time, he runs as best he can, limping into the darkness. For Gordon, Batman has vanished mid-sentence in every meeting, even in daylight. Those events may have spurred a suspicion: Is Batman a higher being?

Seeing him run away is confirmation. Batman was never a god or an angel. He is just a man in black armor, a mere mortal. Gordon says it better to his son:

  • GORDON
  • He's a silent guardian, a watchful protector... a dark knight.
