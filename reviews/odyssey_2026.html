Tanish's Movie Reviews

The Odyssey

July 18, 2026 - 5:22 PM - IMAX Laser

Following the sacker of cities.

Then I defy the Gods.

Around the world, moviegoers will pack IMAX auditoriums with a simple two-word pitch: "Christopher Nolan." He is the writer-director of the moment, operating at the peak of filmmaking power. As the president of the Directors Guild of America, he represents the directorial community in front of every major studio. Yet, he co-opts their superhero-sized financing for his own large-scale, audacious, and original projects. Right now, it certainly feels like everything Christopher Nolan touches turns to gold. And he does not waste opportunities.

Lately, his projects follow an internal playbook:

  1. What will get people into a theater?
  2. Why does this story need to be a film?
  3. Why is this story relevant now?

It's working globally. My local IMAX theater is sold-out for the next two weeks.

Nolan is a realist about cinema's future. With social media and streaming services competing with movie theaters for attention, Nolan creates films that market themselves to an audience, where the act of moviegoing is self-evident. After the stratospheric success of 2023's Oppenheimer, where would he go next?

Homer's The Odyssey is one of the oldest known stories of human civilization, told by rhapsodes to entertain Greek audiences three thousand years ago. Its timeless dramatic core has been reinterpreted into modern tales like 2003's Finding Nemo or 2025's One Battle After Another: Will a family, separated by distance and countless obstacles, be able to reunite?

As a master strategist, he seized the chance for another big at-bat: Traveling the world to shoot on-location, assembling a large A-list cast, filming entirely with IMAX cameras, and adapting Homer's The Odyssey. If successful, it would be an unique achievement in cinema history. Leave it to Nolan and his team, who forge gems where no one has before.

To compress a 500-page epic poem into less than three hours of film, he needed economy of story. Every shot, line of dialogue, and story beat had to be scrutinized, even the Universal Pictures logo.

As The Odyssey begins, we hear bronze gongs ring as we float above an Earth covered in thunderclouds and lightning strikes. The music signifies that we are entering the Bronze Age, when trade emerged between kingdoms. The lightning tells us we are in the presence of the Gods.

  • ODYSSEUS
  • After years of war, no one could stand between my men and home. Not even me.

Nolan has cast the most recognizable faces to guide us. Tom Holland is Telemachus, a boy who is coming-of-age and cannot remember his father. Anne Hathaway plays his mother, Penelope, the Queen of Ithaca, a wife awaiting her king. They have waited 17 years for Odysseus, a wartime general trying to return home. On his way, he sails through the Mediterranean Sea, meets all kinds of mystical creatures, and invites trouble by defying the Gods.

Having read Emily Wilson's English translation before the film, count me impressed by Nolan's editorial decisions. Rather than rushing through everything, he focuses on key events and pulls details from ones he couldn't include. For us fellow-travelers, there are eye-winks in the details like the Lotus-Flowers. The fullness of this world is staggering.

Nolan continues a grand cinema tradition of in-camera innovation for epic films. Stanley Kubrick's Spartacus used Super Technirama 70 in 1960. David Lean's Lawrence of Arabia used Super Panavision 70 in 1962. Those great films of old have new company: Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey is the first narrative feature to film entirely on IMAX film.

Although the IMAX cameras is unwieldy, anywhere from 40 to 600 pounds, Nolan and his team try to use it like any other camera. On set, only one camera is usually rolling at a time. Some of the shots have imperfect focus pulls or feel underexposed – which are kept in the film! It may be a coded message: To experience a good story, the audience will endure. The result is inescapable, an image that towers over the audience, binding us to the story.

The sound design is an all-out assault on our senses. Nolan's sound team captures all the low-end that we hear everyday, but rarely notice. In the world of The Odyssey, the sound of legions yelling, arrows piercing skin, and ships sailing a restless ocean are not just louder in an IMAX; they are fuller and more intense. A wave crashing onto a beach in a Christopher Nolan film sounds different from any other.

In this, Nolan has taken a 3000-year-old story, adapted it for modern times with the best Hollywood techniques, and made it more emotionally resonant.

Like the Trinity Test in Oppenheimer, Nolan likes to have a central set piece, a story-lever that not only drives audiences to IMAX theaters, but acts a point of no return for the characters. In The Odyssey, it is an image of a wooden horse, lodged deep in the sand, with the waves crashing against it: The Trojan Horse.

In Homer's version, the Trojan Horse receives only passing references as Odysseus's greatest achievement, the cause of his renown as the sacker of cities. It is the trick that ended the Trojan War. The horse has seeped into the cultural database, enjoying greater recognition than The Odyssey itself.

By casting Matt Damon as Odysseus, Nolan makes the hubris and cunning of Odysseus approachable. Damon is the everyman actor, who plays relatable heroes. It lures the audience into a protagonist, who might otherwise be unapproachable when taking his decisions at face-value. Nolan's largest surprise, for those familiar with the story, is how the Trojan Horse re-contextualizes Odysseus's journey.

Originally, Odysseus loses all of his men through bad luck, poor decision-making, arrogance, and the wrath of the Gods. His company lands on an island of man-eating giants. All of them, except Odysseus, eat the cattle of the Sun Gods. Odysseus blinds Poseidon's son. In return, Odysseus is jailed on an island as a concubine. He pines to return to his wife, Penelope, and his palace in Ithaca.

For all his actions, the poem character never seeks atonement for any of his dishonorable actions. In his time, Odysseus was not expected to repent. That was seen as a weakness – and his mere survival was a showcase of great strength and skill. Audience expectations have changed; protagonists are hailed for admissions of guilt.

In Nolan's telling, Odysseus and his men struggle through many obstacles. One-by-one, Odysseus loses his men and the weight of the journey grows on him. He wrestles with guilt – how to lead when you cannot guarantee their safety. When they all perish, he is in so much pain that Calypso gives him Lotus-Flowers for seven years to forget his troubles.

Odysseus's men have more agency. You can see the gears turning in their minds, as they look their leader. On two occasions, they threaten mutiny. Damon's Odysseus is more sensitive to them. It is captured in an image: Odysseus stands in front of a grave, with the camera pushing in as he looks pensive. He is reckoning with his guilt, for everything he has done, beginning with the Trojan Horse.

Our introduction to the horse is its glory in battle. Our protagonist launches an ingenious attack: They will beach a massive wooden horse, tell the Trojans that it is a gift from Athena, and they will place it by her temple.

It works. On an IMAX screen, I was enamored by what I saw and heard. Hundreds of men pulling a 35-foot-tall wooden horse over logs. Pulling it up the temple stairs. A city of men and women cheering, elated with this offering from the Gods. They push the horse upright by Athena's temple and celebrate.

At nightfall, the men climb out one-by-one, cramped. They sneak behind the Trojan guards and slit their throats. Odysseus finds the gates and leads his men to open them. It is a race against time. When they open the gates, they yell as one thousand men rush through. The scene floored me.

But it was Odysseus's telling of what followed, that left me devastated. He tells the story to Penelope, appearing as a poor beggar in his own home, referring to himself in the third-person:

  • BEGGAR
  • He saw ten years of rage pour into that city in one night.

We pick up where the first tale finished. The men rush into the city. They grab every Trojan they can find – man, woman, or child – and brutalize them. No one is left alive. Odysseus is surrounded by hundreds of warriors, who fill the screen. Screaming is heard in all directions. We see a city being lit on fire, pushing away the night. This was not military glory, this was murder.

The Trojan Horse was an ingenious military strategy, but it rested on a violation of trust: That such an offering could only be a gift from the Gods. In Homer's The Odyssey, this is xenia, an ancient custom of welcoming, feeding, and sheltering strangers in your home before questioning them. In a world without laws or structure, it was a way of establishing peaceful relations. Nolan recreates it as Zeus's Law.

By using the horse to invade and destroy the city, Odysseus broke this contract between men. Throughout the film, Penelope and Telemachus refer to the "people from the sea" who pillage wherever they land. For poem-readers, our ears went up: "That's new." What we realize is that Odysseus's men are those people. At every encounter, they are unwelcome, attacked on sight, and finally suffer the wrath of the Gods.

In the poem, the Gods speak directly to the characters. Nolan presents them indirectly; we feel the threat of Zeus's thunder and Poseidon's command of the ocean. But there is one exception.

At various times, we see Zendaya join Odysseus and then disappear when something shifts his attention. For poem-readers, the comfortable read is that Zendaya plays Athena, the goddess of strategy who guides the family back together. In the poem, she appears before Odysseus, Telemachus, and Penelope, easing their journey, offering advice, and soothing their pain.

In the film, after his men eat the cattle of the Sun Gods, bringing about their doom, Odysseus tells her,

  • ODYSSEUS
  • You Gods don't speak in ways we understand.
  • ATHENA
  • Who doesn't understand pain or blood? The Gods told you.

We are conditioned to her presence. Then, we find her in a place we least expect. In the bloodbath of Troy, Odysseus summits Athena's temple. There, his men hold a woman hostage. They are ready to behead her. He looks down at her.

It is Athena. Odysseus locks eyes with her. In this moment, he could stop his men. As their general, he could step forward and show mercy. But he stands still and she is beheaded.

Zendaya's character may be a God, haunting Odysseus for breaking Zeus's Law. She may be a Trojan priestess, personifying his guilt. Her true identity is left to the viewer to decide. In all of Nolan's films, meaning is not drawn from the ambiguity itself. It is what Zendaya represents. She is the face that represents Odysseus's mistakes.

Odysseus recounts:

  • BEGGAR
  • To burn the walls of Troy was to burn the world-entire. Including his home.

This line rings familiar because it has a cinematic sister. In Steven Spielberg's Schindler's List, Oskar Schindler is given a gift for saving hundreds of Jews. It is a ring with an inscription from the Talmud: "Whoever saves one life, saves the world-entire." The phrasing is deliberate, because in Nolan's version, he presents the opposing idea. A gift which represents an act of destruction.

The Trojan Horse is Nolan's twist, how he reinterprets Homer's Odyssey for a new age. It becomes the catalyst for everything Odysseus experiences. In the hubris of victory, he and his men pillage villages on the coast. Giants attack his men, rather than welcome them. Circe turns his men to pigs, rather than shelter them. Calypso traps Odysseus with Lotus-Flowers, rather than heal him.

In his home on Ithaca, he keeps his wife and son waiting for 17 years. Suitors invade his home, pillaging his stores of wine, eating his finest cattle, taunting Telemachus, and abusing beggars. Penelope's noble wish to wait for Odysseus, her king, is not respected. Her agency is stolen from her. Even after returning home, Odysseus cannot announce himself; he would be killed on sight. The homecoming music reminds us of the horse.

All of the events turn in meaning. Rather than being unrelated obstacles, each one is caused by a break down of civility. It is our world, after we lose all faith in strangers.

Odysseus's greatest invention, the reason for his fame, becomes his defining nightmare – causing the loss of his men, disharmony in his home, and distrust to spread among the people. Oppenheimer followed a chain reaction that could destroy the world. The Odyssey is its mirror in the Bronze Age: a violation of a sacred bond that ended civilization.

When I left the theater, thunder clouds filled the sky and lightning struck in the distance. After living in the world of The Odyssey, it felt like Zeus was interested in this visit to the IMAX.
