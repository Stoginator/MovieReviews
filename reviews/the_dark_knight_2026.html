Tanish's Movie Reviews

The Dark Knight

September 5, 2026 - 10:05pm - Standard

What do you believe in?

Some men just want to watch the world burn.

Spoilers Ahead

The Dark Knight begins over logos, with the sound of a bat's wings reverberating. The eerie soundstage gives way to an image of blue flame. The Batman's logo pushes towards us, like the character's version of the James Bond gun-barrel. This time, Batman's theme doesn't play; rather, a synthesizer fades in.

BOOM!

A crystal-clear, screen-filling aerial view of downtown Gotham (neé Chicago) appears. The sounds of a city – cars moving, honking, distant sirens – are overpowered by the creepy synthesizer. It doesn't relent until –

POP!

A window shatters. High above the streets, two clowns are up to something, as we hear the gritty sounds of loading a metal arrow launcher. A clown fires it.

CUT TO:

At street-level, the synthesizer returns for a second time, ascending and ascending. The camera pushes in behind a man holding a clown mask. A metro passes in the distance, but no sound survives the synth – until a car screeches to a halt. The man puts on the mask and gets in. The game is on.

As the bank heist begins, the camera directs our eyes towards this clown. He's quiet and observant. There's something different about him.

One by one, the other clowns complete their assignments in pairs: Disabling the alarm, arming civilians with live grenades, and unlocking a vault. However, their final task is to kill their partners – a part of the job that they're happy to complete.

As they each fall, only one remains: The mastermind of this successful and sadistic plot. The synthesizer returns and he unmasks himself. The Joker has arrived.

His musical theme overtaking the city's ambience foreshadows how oppressive his criminal regime will become, even to the Batman. The forceful sound of bat wings cannot undo a high-pitched synthesizer.

  • THE JOKER
  • This is what happens when an unstoppable force meets an immovable object.

The Dark Knight was never a sure bet. When he was asked about a potential sequel to 2005's Batman Begins, writer-director Christopher Nolan played coy. Batman Begins was a realistic take on a comic-book origin story, which firmly planted the Batman mythos in our modern world. The independent filmmaker was nervous about repeating himself.

Yet, Batman Begins constructed a terrific ensemble cast, developed a story world in need of aggressive expansion, and made no premature commitments to the next story – except for one reference: a Joker card found at a crime scene. A sequel had a high ceiling.

To paraphrase his co-writer Jonathan Nolan, how could you refuse the opportunity to take an exceptionally-engineered Lamborghini out for a spin? Christopher Nolan eventually came around, unafraid to raise the stakes and endanger the characters. Everything was on the table, especially the genre and look of the film. The driving question: How do you create a fresh sequel?

Nolan's wisdom in Batman Begins was to play to the audience expectation of a comic-book film in unexpected ways. The film made Gotham City into a character, with corrupted institutions that enable rampant crime and alter the story. For Bruce Wayne (Christian Bale), lawlessness creates the conditions that kill his parents, lead his childhood friend into city law, and transform him into the Batman. At the end, our hero outwits the villain, but the city is fragile. We left hopeful, but wondering: Can Gotham be restored?

The Dark Knight continues exploring the question through "escalation." The Joker is a new threat to the city, whose philosophy of crime cuts deeper into Batman's symbol: What is the proper response to an enemy who is an existential threat? To defeat them, should you change?

  • THE JOKER
  • This city deserves a better class of criminal, and I'm going to give it to them.

Gotham City is plunged further into madness. The film's characters run key institutions of the city, making decisions in a connected ecosystem. When the mob hires the Joker for an ungodly sum, it challenges the police force, the district attorney, the mayor, and Batman to keep the citizens of Gotham safe. Set in a realistic political environment, The Dark Knight becomes a blistering crime film.

To ground Batman's arch-nemesis in reality, The Dark Knight uses generational decay, an idea popularized by HBO's The Wire, where each generation of criminals in the show was more lawless and more ruthless than the last. The Joker's arrival in Gotham is not purely fan-service; there is an emotional and realistic backing to the event.

The incumbent ruling families of Gotham have rules. The Joker doesn't. He is Batman's equal, on the other side of justice. It's evidenced in the opening bank heist, when the mob's bank manager (William Fichtner) lectures the Joker:

  • MANAGER
  • Criminals in this town used to believe in things... Honor. Respect. What do you believe, huh? WHAT DO YOU BELIEVE IN?
  • THE JOKER
  • I believe that what doesn't kill you simply makes you... stranger.

Heath Ledger plays this anarchic version of the character. Compared to his roles in The Patriot, A Knight's Tale, Brokeback Mountain, he's unrecognizable. The white, black, and red makeup removes our traditional access point for a character. It becomes a mask that represents the Joker's inhumanity. His schemes demand such extraordinary measures – that it is a surprise to learn the Joker is only onscreen for one-fifth of the film. When things start to go bad, there feels like no floor. His evil is uncomfortable and all-enveloping. Ledger's performance, which is the stuff of legend, enables the character to leap into our psyche. He's there even when he's not.

To intensify the threat of the Joker, Gotham had to be imbued with the feeling of a real city. Its vastness is presented with IMAX-sized views of skylines and towering skyscrapers. Scenes take place in on rooftops, top-floor boardrooms, in Bruce's penthouse, or with the city seen through windows. This fictional setting feels like an American city.

❮
❯

Aside from Batman, the city has only one legitimate, saving grace: Harvey Dent (Aaron Eckhart), the political poster-child of renewal. For a city desperate for the rule of law, he is a dream come true. A fresh face, symbolizing fearlessness and optimism. He's one of the bulwarks that the Joker will have to bring down.

Like Batman Begins before it, The Dark Knight runs heavy on philosophy, but not in an academic way. It's intimated from action and dialogue. The Joker believes the natural order is disorder. Everything that men have created – government, hospitals, roads, cities – are his playground to disrupt and burn. He truly believes that the makings of civilization hide our true nature: Cruel, vile, and evil. He wants you to think he's shallow, but he isn't.

  • THE JOKER
  • Do I really look like a guy with a plan?

The Joker shares two stories from his personal life, where members of his family are mutilated by knifes. In one story, he harms himself with a knife to prove something to his wife:

  • THE JOKER
  • I just want her to know I don't care about the scars.

Whether they are true or not, they feed his narrative that the world is cruel. His worldview is immutable. While he uses violence to coerce others, the Joker prefers to place his victims in horrible situations – or, more difficult, pitch them on his extremist beliefs. When he begins to speak, watch out. You don't know who he will convince.

The Joker pitches three audiences on his philosophy: Gotham City, Batman, and Harvey Dent. His pitches to Gotham City are blackmail:

  • THE JOKER
  • Batman must take off his mask, and turn himself in. Oh and every day he doesn't, people will die. Starting tonight. I'm a man of my word.

He applies pressure to the city by building the circumstances to drive true behavior, as he sees it: unrest and lawlessness. By mid-way of the film, he starts to get it. Citizens of Gotham are faced with wretched decisions at the whims of a terrorist: Do I kill one man to save my wife in the hospital?

For all the visible space in Gotham, we start to feel more claustrophobic. More attacks, like the bank heist or hospital explosion, happen in broad daylight, a time when the Batman cannot respond. The Joker is unafraid of being seen. As order collapses, we feel this terrorist closing in on an American city – and there is no escape until the end.

With his plan wreaking havoc in the streets, he feels emboldened enough to pitch the Batman. In the famous interrogation scene between them, the villain wastes no time beginning his pitch. He's going to take a swing at the Batman's philosophy. And, if words won't convince him, his plan will force him to break his one rule.

  • BATMAN
  • You're garbage who kills for money.
  • THE JOKER
  • Don't talk like one of them – you're not, even if you'd like to be.

What's the Joker's play? Convince Batman that he has more in common with me than the police. Here, he offers the clearest indication of his true goal:

  • THE JOKER
  • You'll see. I'll show you... when the chips are down, these civilized people... they'll eat each other.

But Batman is incensed at his worldview, driving him into fury. He levels the Joker with punches, but each one deals the opposite effect: The Joker enjoys the beating, laughing after each one.

  • THE JOKER
  • You have nothing to threaten me with. Nothing to do with all your strength.

The Joker's pitch fails on the Batman, but he takes rejection very well. He has strategically set up his next pitch for Harvey Dent, not at the peak of his power, but at his most vulnerable. Having killed Dent's blushing bride-to-be and burned half of his face, the Joker tries to prove that the events weren't his fault.

  • THE JOKER
  • Do I really look like a guy with a plan?

Dent initially resists, but the hospital has strapped him to the bed. He can't help but listen.

  • THE JOKER
  • Look what I've done to this city with a few drums of gas and a couple of bullets.

The wheels turn in Dent's head. He wants someone to explain his trauma. No one in the city could contain the Joker. They all failed him, especially the Batman.

  • THE JOKER
  • I'm an agent of chaos. And you know the thing about chaos, Harvey? It's fair.

With that, whatever faith Dent had in the system is gone. He realizes that the structure of a civilized world is not enough to contain uncivilized men, like the Joker. His pain leads him to repudiate what he was once part of: the justice system.

  • DENT
  • The Joker's just a mad dog. I want whoever let him off the leash.

Dent becomes a vigilante to deal vengeance in a way the justice system never could. He flips a coin to determine whether to executes his enemies, like the mob boss, Falcone, or the cops who betrayed him. The Joker convinces Dent that the system's frailty is why Rachel died.

The Joker's pitch succeeds, destroying Dent's little remaining faith in Gotham. He turns Gotham's symbol of political renewal into a new symbol of its disillusionment. It is here that we learn the arc of The Dark Knight runs through Harvey Dent, who personifies how crushing the Joker's tests are on Gotham City.

To find the Joker, Batman puts together a system of mass surveillance, listening in on every cell phone in the city. The CEO of Wayne Enterprises, Lucius Fox (Morgan Freeman), cannot believe his eyes:

  • FOX
  • Beautiful. Unethical. Dangerous. You've turned every phone in the city into a microphone...
  • BATMAN
  • I've got to find this man, Lucius.
  • FOX
  • But at what cost?

The Batman has one rule: Don't kill. But his one rule isn't comprehensive enough to be useful in a modern society: Should he invade the privacy of millions of citizens to locate a terrorist?

His decision makes us realize: Batman is unaccountable. He is a form of one-man rule with concentrated power. His decisions have cost lives. So far, we've agreed with him, but what happens when he makes unjustifiable decisions? The Joker pushes every character to their limit.

The film hurtles towards a final confrontation between Batman, Dent, and Jim Gordon (Gary Oldman), where the three debate the circumstances that the Joker has put them in. Dent has a gun to Gordon's son, ready to pull the trigger based on the flip of a coin. He wants to replicate what the loss of Rachel felt like.

Batman arrives to nullify the situation. But he fails. The Joker's philosophy has totally won over Dent. To save Gordon's son, Batman takes them both over a ledge. Dent falls many stories, crystalizing his fall from grace, but the boy is saved. Lacking the strength to pull himself up, Batman falls the same way.

We see these two fallen men laying next to each other: one masked in matte black armor and the other maskless in a burnt suit. Their roles are reversed: Batman is Gotham's incorruptible hero, while Dent is now its corrupted symbol of hope.

Gordon helps Batman up – and we see Batman crouch again to turn Dent's head to his good side.

  • BATMAN
  • The Joker cannot win.

The gears turn in Batman's head – until he says:

  • BATMAN
  • I killed those people. That's what I can be.

To save Gotham City, Batman will assume responsibility for Dent's actions. He justifies telling Gotham City the lie:

  • BATMAN
  • Because that's what needs to happen. Because, sometimes, the truth isn't good enough. Sometimes people deserve more. Sometimes, people deserve to have their faith rewarded.

In a few sentences, Batman explains the promise and the peril of political movements – both real and fictional. Harvey Dent was supposed to be Gotham's savior: "I believe in Harvey Dent" was the people's slogan. If the truth of his demise became known, it could irreversibly break the city's spirits. Their optimism may never return. To spare them, the lie is the only option.

Batman's decision is a Pyrrhic victory. To win, he must sacrifice his symbol and exile himself. The Batman will no longer be there to save the day, even though Gotham still needs him.

Exile reverses the creeping one-man rule of the Batman. The Batman has amassed tremendous power in Gotham, thrashing her streets at night, surveilling her citizens, and using military-grade tools against criminality. It is too much power for one man. Through exile, Batman makes the democratic choice, returning power to the people and their accountable, political institutions. Yet, for all his heroism, he will be tainted by the lie.

This choice is a characteristic, final destination for Christopher Nolan, who tends to wrap up each thread in his stories one-by-one in a terminal sequence. His characters never achieve a full, unvarnished victory. Neither does the Batman. And neither do Gordon or the city. What will they do without him?

With the decision made, it's time for Batman to leave. However, his exit isn't a disappearance like usual. This time, he runs as best he can, limping into the darkness.

For Gordon, imagine someone who seemingly disappears on command, even in daylight. Those events may have spurred questions: Is Batman a higher being?

Seeing him run away is confirmation. Batman was never a God or an angel. He is just a man in black armor. Gordon says it better to his son:

  • GORDON
  • He's a silent guardian, a watchful protector... a dark knight.
