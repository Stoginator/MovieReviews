Tanish's Movie Reviews

Network

August 8, 2026 - 8:48 AM - Standard

The downward spiral of unchecked incentives.

All necessities provided, all anxieties tranquilized, all boredom amused.

Spoilers Ahead

In its day, the response to Network by Paddy Chayefsky was immediate. Earning 4 Academy Awards, the audience of 1976 connected to it. For a country reeling from tumultuous times – protest, assassinations, Vietnam, Watergate – a story about a news organization that trades proper reporting for entertainment and profit, losing all its nobility, was no longer startling. Add it to the list.

Network's director, Sidney Lumet, considers the picture to be a satirical black comedy. I must admit I didn't laugh much. I was dumbfounded.

Fifty years later, this film, filled with monologues to end all monologues, has not lost its sway. Everything these characters shout remains relevant. So much so that I see Network as a tragedy: In our system of limitless potential, Chayefsky finds our fatal flaw. When America's powerful, free-market incentives are left unchecked, they can cause our undoing.

At its core, Network is an intellectual film about a society that only knows TV Land: Where life is expected to resemble a script; where real human emotion should be escapable through an ad break; where truly heinous actions are shrugged off — or worse, mass entertainment.

These ideas are expressed through monologue after monologue, which never become redundant or stale. They are dramatically exhilarating. They fit their characters’ desires, as they battle the obstacles before them. Yet, they also present the voice of the film’s author, Paddy Chayefsky. Using his characters, he speaks to his film’s themes directly. He breaks a classical rule: Characters are not a bullhorn for a writer’s beliefs. But these monologues are written gold.

Until the late 70s, news divisions in media companies were loss-leaders. Reporting the news was seen as a public service, an acceptable place to be "in the red" on a corporate balance sheet. What if new divisions were profitable? With the incentives realigned around how we get our information, what would become of America?

Network is a film about a group of people who stumble into an immensely profitable nightly news program. Their discovery is a mentally-ill news host named Howard Beale (Peter Finch) who galvanizes America with his rage-filled, spontaneous monologues. They are authentic, timely, and uncensored. They come from a man who clearly needs care. Make no mistake: He won't receive any; this is primetime television we're talking about. Beale begins to make his corporation too much money. He's much too valuable to be treated.

A healthy organization would ask, "Do we sacrifice our good natured news programming to feature profane monologues?" The internal debate at his station, UBS, is short-lived. When TV ad revenue expands and the spigot of money appears, ambitious executives rush in to create a firehose. The organization completely realigns. Anyone who stands opposed will be eviscerated. Principle, you say? In an economic recession, principles are expensive. That's what the conniving executives know.

There's Frank Hackett (Robert Duvall), whose identity is tied to his position on the org chart. Laser-focused on earnings, he deftly looks for opportunities to make his superiors look good – by making them more money. He is loyal only to those with more power. If he smells blood, he'll wield power absolutely. For Hackett, the equivalent of a death sentence is to lose his corporate standing.

  • HACKETT
  • Four hours ago, I was the Sun God at CCA, Mr. Jensen's handpicked golden boy, the heir apparent. Now I'm a man without a corporation.

Diana Christensen (Faye Dunaway) is the woman who emboldens him, a creature who thrives in TV Land. It is all she knows. She is soulless, repellant to long-term relationships, immune to human pleas, and incapable of love. Work is all she can do. Television is her religion. She will serve it at all costs.

  • DIANA
  • All I want out of life is a 30 share and a 20 rating.

By pure description, Diana must be a villain. After all, she is afforded the most screen time and Dunaway has top-billing. Yet, this never manifests because Dunaway successfully walks a dramatic tight rope. She makes Diana accessible, by shifting our anger away from her character towards the TV Land system she was born in: Where life is television. What human decency can we expect from those who consumed sex, drugs, and violence from their first days? They don't know any better. She represents the next generation: harder, unprincipled, and self-serving.

Frank Hackett and Diana Christensen represent a changing of the guard. So who's on the out? The current news division president, Max Schumacher (William Holden). Learning the ropes during the Golden Age of Journalism, he carries ideals about what news can be: decent, fair, truth-seeking. It's no coincidence that the actor portraying him, William Holden, rose to fame during the Golden Age of Hollywood. Subconsciously, we know what he represents.

Being such a talky film on paper, a natural question may be: "Is this material better suited to a stage or a screen?" When these characters clash, director Sidney Lumet is our guide, placing the camera in the right places to highlight the right reactions. We follow the character beats so effortlessly, that we may take this invisible feat for granted. With Lumet's direction, Network's medium becomes self-evident.

Once Schumacher is out, the business norms collapse. Television news becomes gilded. It's shiny and glamorized, but completely debased of its original purpose. A mentally ill man is broadcast daily into millions of American homes. Social norms and laws are next to fall – as appalling events are premeditated. Fear not, they'll say, it's all show-business. When this inevitably happens, our capacity for bewilderment is gone. We are desensitized.

To capitalize on business opportunity, Hackett and Christensen open Pandora's Box. They unleash demons by spewing rage on national television. New Yorkers become angrier; what of our suburbs and rural lands? What will more rage do to our Thanksgivings, our workplaces, or our political system?

Network focuses on the original television metrics for gauging attention: audience share and ratings. The business obsession and monetization of attention hasn't diminished; we call it likes, clicks, and views. The names and venues have changed, but the behavior is the same.

Social consequences aren't priced into their profit-and-loss statements. These characters don't consider them. But, even if they did, we'd discover something even more damming: They wouldn't care. It would be the cost of doing business. With smiling faces, they'd say, "Keep the firehose running."
