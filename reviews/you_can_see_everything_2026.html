You Can See Everything

October 17, 2026 - 5:45 PM - Standard

Which story do you believe?

I'm always being real.

Spoilers Ahead

The opening shot of You Can See Everything sets the tone. Laying on the floor, a father discusses how the media made his partner into a monster. Behind him, she listens while holding their newborn son.

It’s Elizabeth Holmes, presented in a way we've never seen her, trying to be a normal mom. The camera is behind a crib, depicting her behind its smooth bars. The shot is a prelude: In thirty-four days, Elizabeth Holmes will be incarcerated. You Can See Everything promises to show us a portrait of a woman who captivated the world as a role model — and then as a villain.

Elizabeth Holmes's main talent is storytelling. For years, she created a mythology around herself and her defunct company, Theranos. The media described her as Steve Jobs's heir apparent. As a woman entrepreneur in the male-dominated tech industry, Elizabeth was "making health more accessible" with the proprietary blood test system at Theranos.

The perception was far from the truth. The company's products fell so far below what was promised that it was effectively a sham. In a criminal proceeding, an impartial jury found Elizabeth knew her products did not work, exaggerated facts about their adoption, and knowingly solicited investor funding using falsified information. For this, she was sentenced to 11.25 years in jail.

Facts can take you only so far. Walking into Elizabeth’s home is another experience altogether. Why would she invite a film crew to document her life before imprisonment? What could she tell us to make us doubt the guilty verdict of an impartial jury?

In her post-Theranos era, Elizabeth has a new style that disarms us. No more black turtlenecks, red lipstick, or a phony, masculine voice. The new look is a sweater, jeans, and zero makeup. Now, she’s a mother of two, nursing a newborn, with an endearing nickname: “Liz.” Elizabeth's partner, Billy Evans, and her kids are her support system. She's not the character we remember.

Nathan Fielder is the couple’s handpicked interviewer, a comedian who uses uncomfortable moments to push people from politeness to brutal honesty. Nathan makes repeated attempts to understand Elizabeth’s past, even calling her “Liz.” But his questions are searing. How would you react if you were asked:

  1. You really feel you did nothing wrong?
  2. You’ve never been suspicious that she’s ever lied to you?
  3. Weren’t you worried that she was scamming you?

Indignant, powerless, apathetic? The emotions that I expected to see are nowhere to be found. Co-director Lance Oppenheim's preference for long-held close-ups reveal that Elizabeth and Billy have accepted the reality of their predicament. A young mother is going to jail for eleven years, leaving behind a father to raise their kids alone.

Their conversations aren't all investigatory; at times, there's a friendliness. Elizabeth asks Nathan if he wants to go swimming on the beach. He declines. For Elizabeth, it's Nathan loss as swimming helps clear her mind. When Nathan doubts whether he is seeing everything, Elizabeth reveals a sense of humor through a text.

  • LIZ
  • FYI you can totally see everything.

Against these moments, I would remember: Elizabeth Holmes was convicted by an impartial jury. I felt the emotional whiplash of my sympathy darting back and forth like a ping-pong.

Billy calls these interviews an act of “radical transparency.” In a way, it's true. The film crew has complete access to the couple during waking hours. Nathan can ask anything he wants. They present an image of a happy family being unfairly ripped apart.

Yet, their message to Nathan is identical to their legal arguments: Elizabeth and Theranos are misunderstood. The media misinterpreted her statements. She never meant to imply that her products functioned, only that they would eventually.

There's more. Elizabeth describes the many forms of abuse she received from Theranos’ former COO, Sunny Balwani. At Theranos, he forced her to do things against her will. Elizabeth was also in a romantic relationship with him. Her father matter-of-factly mentions that Elizabeth's life changed completely after Sunny raped her. Was Elizabeth co-opted by a domineering man taking advantage of her position?

Elizabeth's story casts herself as the ultimate victim: controlled by an abusive man at home and work, misrepresented by the media, and not given enough time to prove her product. She sincerely believes that she did nothing wrong. She wants to convince us that her truth is the truth.

Confronted by her story, I started to doubt my understanding of the history. Did I fall prey to the mainstream narrative about a misunderstood entrepreneur?

In the face of her emotion, the facts fell away, even with all my skepticism. What surfaced were two mutually exclusive stories:

  1. Our institutions (investors, the media, the government) failed Elizabeth Holmes, sending an innocent mother to jail for crimes which were taken out of context.
  2. Elizabeth Holmes is self-delusional. She has reconstructed reality to make herself the ultimate victim, preyed on by powerful men, our institutions, and the public.

To paraphrase Billy: If the skeptics won’t believe you anyway, all you can do is tell your truth. Working with a film crew allowed Elizabeth to escape the confines of a courtroom and directly plead her case to the general public.

Her greatest talent is storytelling. She knows how to present an emotional truth that outweighs the facts. She is media-savvy, having constructed the Theranos persona and using it across hundreds of interviews. To reclaim credibility, her final destination is an audience in the court of public opinion.

Nathan Fielder is the wild card they underestimated. Like us, he does not know what he has experienced in the couple's home. He wrestles with the difficulty of uncovering the truth, without the ability to see into someone's mind. It's the paradox of the title, You Can See Everything. Can we ever know which story is true?

After Elizabeth is imprisoned, Nathan brings in Amanda Seyfried, an actress who studied Elizabeth's story. With her, Nathan stages reenactments with Seyfried as Holmes to re-present what happened to him. He is trying to process his memories.

In Elizabeth's absence, Billy's level of control over her life comes into focus. He has power of attorney for Elizabeth. He controls her Twitter feed, which was not publicly known at the time. His statements on camera are vengeful about the people who led the charge against Elizabeth. He oversees who Elizabeth talks to outside of prison. Ultimately, he starts to demand more creative control over the film, rupturing his relationship with Nathan.

Billy resembles the domineering man that Elizabeth's former COO is alleged to be. It's the kind of man that Elizabeth seems to attract and accept. Because Billy seems like another bad guy, does Elizabeth need to be protected from him? Or is it a larger ploy: Is Billy the one who does the dirty work, so Elizabeth can keep her hands clean?

Nathan reveals that Billy requested backend profits from A24 before the project started, a massive departure from the norm. Before any discussion of profits, Elizabeth leaves the room. Her timing is suspiciously convenient. Did she know what was going to happen?

When handling these defining questions, You Can See Everything never cheats the audience. We understand there are two sides of every story. Billy's two kids miss their mother every day, yet they appear happy and healthy. He seems like a good father. Are we only seeing the side of Billy that looks bad?

With Billy looming over the film, Nathan tries to get access to Elizabeth in jail one last time to determine if she knew about the profit-sharing agreement. If she did, it would signal that they perpetrated a scam on Nathan. Their house interviews were a coordinated scheme. If she didn't, it would tell us that Billy is a continuation of bad men in her life. No cameras are allowed in jail, so Nathan's recollection is all we have.

At first, Nathan remembers Elizabeth indirectly confirm her knowledge of A24's profit-sharing agreement. Then, he scratches that thought and replaces it: She didn't know about the agreement specifically. Just discussions she was not privy to.

This is where You Can See Everything hits a fundamental limit: People’s thoughts are the last private space. From the outside, thoughts are unknowable. We will never know why Nathan corrects his original statement and which version is closer to the truth. But there is a clue.

Amanda Seyfried accuses Nathan of being too close to Elizabeth. Then, in the most shocking reveal of the film, Nathan is shown swimming with Liz in the ocean. Nathan originally declines Liz's offer onscreen to maintain his objectivity. His reversal is not shown.

In the house, Nathan is the audience in the rooms with Elizabeth and Billy. To represent us, he must ask the hard-hitting questions we are wondering. The reveal forces us to rethink everything we have seen. If Nathan is emotionally compromised and feeling sympathy for Elizabeth, it could shift his memories of Elizabeth into a more appealing light. In the second half of the film, Amanda Seyfried becomes the audience.

His lack of objectivity means he never asks Elizabeth the defining question of her story: If someone is in very bad circumstances, at what point are they responsible for their actions?

We will never know if Elizabeth Holmes is sincere or not in her stated innocence. The truth rests with her. Yet, the more time we spend with someone, the more we normalize their behavior. Nathan falls into a human trap and takes the audience with him.

When the film ended, I left feeling unmoored. The film's unsettling score was rattling around my brain. I expected this film to showcase the shortcomings of Elizabeth Holmes, not our system. To believe Elizabeth's story, you must believe that the media, the government, and the justice system are all broken. Could our institutions have failed so onerously, subjecting a mother to a certain hell?

Then, I realized: I fell prey to Elizabeth’s reality distortion field like thousands of others. I returned to the facts of her criminal conviction. She knowingly lied to investors about what her products could do. She ran her company in a way that silenced dissent, siloed departments, and disillusioned young, idealistic employees. Comparing her emotional truth to the facts, I felt deceived.

Elizabeth's reality distortion field hijacks our perception to show us what we want to see. It's what convinced former presidents, moguls, and four-star generals to believe in her vision. In her presence, her stories feel real. You Can See Everything is an important historical document of Elizabeth's power to sway anyone in front of her.

We are hard-wired to recognize emotion over facts. When someone is passionately voicing something into existence, it is difficult to dismiss them.

  • LIZ
  • I remain completely committed to my dream of making affordable healthcare solutions available to everyone.

Wake up! At Theranos, there was never anything there. All of it was an illusion. Sitting across from her, she will spin a web that makes you forget that her magic trick hurt far more people than it ever helped.
