You Can See Everything

October 17, 2026 - 5:45 PM - Standard

Which story do you believe?

I'm always being real.

Spoilers Ahead

The opening shot of You Can See Everything sets the tone. Laying on the floor, a father discusses how the media made his partner into a monster. Behind him, she listens while holding their newborn son.

It’s Elizabeth Holmes, presented in a way we've never seen her, trying to be a normal mom. The camera is behind a crib, depicting her behind its smooth bars. The shot is a prelude: In thirty-four days, Elizabeth Holmes will be incarcerated. You Can See Everything promises to show us a portrait of a woman who captivated the world as a role model — and then as a villain.

Elizabeth Holmes's main talent is storytelling. For years, she created a mythology around herself and her defunct company, Theranos. The media identified her as Steve Jobs's heir apparent. As a woman entrepreneur in the male-dominated tech industry, Elizabeth was "making health more accessible" with its proprietary blood test system.

The perception was far from the truth. The company's products fell so far below what was promised that it was effectively a sham. In a criminal proceeding, an impartial jury found Elizabeth knew her products did not work, exaggerated facts about their adoption, and knowingly solicited investor funding using falsified information. For this, she was sentenced to 11.25 years in jail.

Facts can take you only so far. Walking into Elizabeth’s home is another experience altogether. Why would she invite a film crew to document her life before imprisonment? What could she tell us to make us doubt the guilty verdict of an impartial jury?

In her post-Theranos era, Elizabeth has a new style that disarms us. No more black turtlenecks, red lipstick, or a phony, masculine voice. The new look is a sweater, jeans, and zero makeup. Now, she’s a mother of two, nursing a newborn, with an endearing nickname: “Liz.” Elizabeth's partner, Billy Evans, and her kids are her support system. She's not the character we remember.

Nathan Fielder is the couple’s handpicked interviewer, a comedian who uses uncomfortable moments to push people from politeness to brutal honesty. Fielder makes repeated attempts to understand Elizabeth’s past, even calling her “Liz.” But his questions are searing. How would you react if you were asked:

  1. You really feel you did nothing wrong?
  2. You’ve never been suspicious that she’s ever lied to you?
  3. Weren’t you worried that she was scamming you?

Indignant, powerless, apathetic? The emotions that I expected to see are nowhere to be found. Co-director Lance Oppenheim's preference for long-held close-ups reveal that Elizabeth and Billy have accepted the reality of their predicament. A young mother is going to jail for eleven years, leaving behind a father to raise their kids away from her.

Their conversations aren't all investigatory; at times, there's a friendliness. Elizabeth asks Nathan if he wants to go swimming on the beach. He declines. His loss: Swimming helps clear her mind. When Nathan doubts whether he is seeing everything, Elizabeth reveals a sense of humor through a text.

  • LIZ
  • FYI you can totally see everything.

Against these moments, I would remember: Elizabeth Holmes was convicted by an impartial jury. It was a sensation of emotional whiplash, of my sympathy darting back and forth like a ping-pong.

Billy calls these interviews an act of “radical transparency.” In a way, it's true. The film crew has complete access to the couple during waking hours. Fielder can ask anything he wants. They present an image of a happy family being unfairly ripped apart.

Their message to Fielder is identical to their legal arguments: Elizabeth and Theranos are misunderstood. The media misinterpreted her statements. She never meant to imply that her products functioned, only that they would eventually.

There's more. Elizabeth describes Theranos’ former COO, Sunny Balwani. At Theranos, he forced her to do things against her will. Elizabeth was in a romantic relationship with him and he abused her. In a shock, her father mentions that he sexually assaulted her. Was Elizabeth co-opted by a greedy, vengeful, and domineering man taking advantage of her position?

Her story casts herself as the ultimate victim: controlled by an abusive man at home and work, misrepresented by the media, and not given enough time to prove her product. Elizabeth sincerely believes that she did nothing wrong. She wants to convince us that her truth is the truth.

Confronted by her story, I started to doubt my understanding of the history. Did I fall prey to the mainstream narrative about a misunderstood entrepreneur? Still, I wish Nathan asked her another question: If someone is in very bad circumstances, at what point are they responsible for their actions?

In the face of human emotion, the facts fell away, even with all my skepticism. There are two mutually exclusive stories:

  1. Our institutions (investors, the media, the government) failed Elizabeth Holmes, sending an innocent mother to jail for crimes which were taken out of context.
  2. Elizabeth Holmes is self-delusional. She has reconstructed reality to make herself the ultimate victim, preyed on by powerful men, our institutions, and the public.

To paraphrase Billy: If the skeptics won’t believe you anyway, all you can do is tell your truth. Working with a film crew allowed Elizabeth to escape the confines of a courtroom and directly plead her case to the general public.

Her greatest talent is storytelling. She knows how to present an emotional truth that outweighs the facts. She is media-savvy, having constructed the Theranos persona and using it across hundreds of interviews. To reclaim credibility, she wants an audience in the court of public opinion.

Nathan Fielder is the wild card they underestimated. Like us, he does not know what he has experienced in the couple's home. He wrestles with the difficulty of uncovering the truth, without the ability to see into someone's mind. It's the paradox of the title, You Can See Everything. Can we ever know which story is true?

After Elizabeth is imprisoned, Fielder brings in Amanda Seyfried, an actress who studied Elizabeth's story. He processes his memories, staging reenactments with Seyfried as Holmes to present what happened to him.

In Elizabeth's absence, Fielder stays in touch with Billy, whose image begins to unravel. Billy starts to demand more creative control over the film. He reveals his power of attorney over Elizabeth and control of her Twitter feed, without alerting the public. His statements on camera are vengeful. Billy controls Elizabeth's communications in and out of prison.

Billy resembles a domineering man that Elizabeth's COO is alleged to be. It's the kind of man that Elizabeth seems to attract and accept. Is Billy the one who does the dirty work, so Elizabeth can keep her hands clean? We must remember: There are two sides of every story. Are we only seeing the side of Billy that looks bad? For missing their mother every day, his kids appear healthy and happy.

Then, Nathan reveals that Billy requested backend profits from A24 for the project to ever start, a massive departure from the norm.

With Billy looming over the film, Fielder tries to get access to Elizabeth in jail one last time. Did she know about the profit-sharing agreement? If she did, it would tell us that they perpetrated a scam on Fielder. Their house interviews were a coordinated scheme. If she didn't, it would tell us that Billy does not have Elizabeth's best interests at heart. No cameras are allowed in jail, so Nathan's recollection is all we have.

At first, Nathan remembers Elizabeth indirectly confirm her knowledge of A24's profit-sharing agreement. Then, he scratches that thought and replaces it: She didn't know about the agreement. Amanda Seyfried accuses him of being too close to Elizabeth.

This is where You Can See Everything hits a fundamental limit: People’s thoughts are our last private space. From the outside, thoughts are unknowable. We will never know why Nathan corrects his original statement and whether it is closer to the truth. But there is a clue.

In the most shocking reveal of the film, Nathan is shown swimming with Liz in the ocean. Nathan originally declines Liz's offer onscreen to maintain his objectivity. His reversal is not shown.

Throughout the film, Nathan is the audience in the rooms with Elizabeth and Billy. To represent us, he must ask the hard-hitting questions we are wondering. The reveal forces us to rethink everything we have seen. If Nathan is emotionally compromised and feeling sympathy for Elizabeth, it could shift his memories of Elizabeth into a more appealing light. In the second half of the film, Amanda Seyfried becomes the audience.

We will never know if Elizabeth Holmes is sincere or not in her stated innocence. The truth rests with her. Yet, the more time we spend with someone, the more we normalize their behavior. Nathan fell into a human trap. So did I.

When the film ended, I left feeling unmoored. The film's unsettling score was rattling around my brain. I expected this film to showcase the shortcomings of Elizabeth Holmes, not our system. To believe Elizabeth's story, you must believe that the media, the government, and the justice system are all broken. Could our institutions have failed so onerously, subjecting a mother to a certain hell?

Then, I realized: I fell prey to Elizabeth’s reality distortion field. I returned to the facts of her criminal conviction. She knowingly lied to investors about what her products could do. She ran her company in a way that silenced dissent and disillusioned young, idealistic employees. Comparing her emotional truth and the facts, I felt deceived.

Now, I understood the experience of Elizabeth Holmes's reality distortion field. She hijacks our perception to show us what we want to see. It's what convinced former presidents, moguls, and four-star generals to believe in her vision. In her presence, her stories feel real. You Can See Everything is an important historical document of Elizabeth's power to sway anyone in front of her.

We are hard-wired to recognize emotion over facts. When someone is desperately voicing something into existence, it is difficult to dismiss them.

Wake up! At Theranos, there was never anything there. All of it was the brilliant magic trick of Elizabeth Holmes.
