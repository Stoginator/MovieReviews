Once Upon a Time... in Hollywood

November 1, 2026 - 10:10 PM - IMAX Laser

A magnificent tone.

My booze don't need no buddy.

Spoilers Ahead

The first time I watched Once Upon a Time in Hollywood, I was completely lost. Unlike most Angelinos, I had never heard of Charles Manson, Sharon Tate, or Cielo Drive. For them, the murder of Sharon Tate and her friends by the Manson family (i.e., radicalized hippies) is a well-known tragedy. They weren't part of this Pittsburgh kid’s lexicon.

On that first viewing, the destination was unknown for most of its three-hour runtime. I wondered, had writer-director Quentin Tarantino finally missed, even armed with three movie stars, Leonardo DiCaprio, Brad Pitt, and Margot Robbie? It was equal parts meandering and exciting, which sustained my attention.

When the now-iconic sequence arrives with four hippies cruising up Cielo Drive in the moonlight, my patience was rewarded. These real hippies are about to meet Tarantino’s fictional creations, altering the course of history. In the darkness of the theater, all was forgiven.

In their scrap car, the hippies drive past Cliff Booth (Brad Pitt), a stunt double walking his dog and smoking an acid-soaked cigarette. He doesn’t give them a thought.

They reach the cul-de-sac where Rick Dalton (Leonardo DiCaprio), a “has-been” TV actor, is blending frozen margaritas in his home. Over the blender, he hears the low-hum of an unmuffled vehicle outside.

  • RICK
  • F**king private road. Damn property taxes up the butt.
  • (looks out window)
  • Bunch of goddamn f**kin’ hippies.

Rick marches outside to confront four members of the Manson Family including Tex Watson and Susan Atkins.

  • RICK
  • You don’t belong here. Now take this mechanical asshole and get it off my f**king street!

As Rick lays into them, we see Tex holding a revolver at his side. They’re here to kill.

The scene’s wild tone is pure Tarantino. In his pleasurable rewriting of history, he alternates between Rick’s humorous disdain for hippies and our fear of their intentions. Just wait, it gets better.

On his way to the film’s climax, Tarantino immerses us into Los Angeles, 1969. Real radio excerpts from 93 KHJ play in the background. Contemporary songs (Neil Diamond) and advertisements ("Numero Uno!") become the film’s soundtrack. Neon signs and vintage cars fill LA’s streets and highways. The sounds and sights of the times are beautifully recreated.

❯

In the film’s look and feel, there’s a classic sensibility. The film is filled with two-shots, forcing us to pick the which character to look at, like films of that era. Every character has their share of long takes: tracking shots of Cliff driving through LA, crane shots of Rick walking through a television set, close-ups of Sharon Tate (Margot Robbie) enjoying her own scenes in a movie theater. On my first watch, these moments meandered. Every time after, they are pure joy.

In Tarantino’s words, this is a hangout movie. The more we hang out with Rick, Cliff, and Sharon, the more we don’t want to leave. Each one earns their keep. Tarantino successfully steals the flavor of a television serial while keeping the satisfying structure of a film.

Of all of Tarantino’s creations, Rick Dalton is my favorite. Rick operates on a wild pendulum of immense swagger to crushing self-doubt. He'd be annoying if he wasn't so goddamn funny. He aspires to get leading parts in an industry he loves, only to get rebuffed and settle for the villain. To cope with playing "the heavy," he drinks. He’s an addled alcoholic, but he’s my guy:

  • RICK
  • If coming face-to-face with the failure that is your career ain’t worth crying about, then I don’t know what the f**k is.

Leonardo DiCaprio imbues Rick’s emotional state into his body language. When Rick’s uncertainty weighs heavy, Rick sulks, stutters, and chain-smokes Red Apples. In fleeting moments of hope and opportunity, Rick stands tall, with his shoulders back, and motions with his arms.

  • RICK
  • Hollywood real estate means you live here. You’re not just visiting, not just passing through. You f**king live here.

Tarantino’s dialogue for Rick flows so well that it’s almost musical. For Rick, a Missourian in LA like a fish out of water, DiCaprio uses a slight Southern twang that makes his lines melodic. Great lines abound in Once Upon a Time in Hollywood, but the combination of Leonardo DiCaprio and Rick Dalton makes every line special:

  • RICK
  • I’m the horse’s ass that got Bounty Law cancelled because I wanted some f**kin’ rinky-dink movie career.

Even the lines of the heavy (Caleb DeCoteau) that Rick plays are wonderful.

  • CALEB
  • I just want her to play the fiddle. Now go tell her I’ll give her a fat five dollar piece to play her little chili pepper heart out!

DiCaprio knew Rick so well that he improvised a massive departure from the script. In Hollywood, Tarantino's scripts are like religious objects, forbidding changes. When Rick plays Caleb DeCoteau, DiCaprio proposed that Rick should forget his lines in front of everyone and freak-out at himself in his trailer. Tarantino was respectful of his lead actor's request, so he agreed to film it (i.e., burn cash on a film set). When he saw the first take, Tarantino decided that his script needed to change.

With the change, Rick has a beautiful arc. He bottoms out on a television set, confronts himself in his trailer, and returns to perform at the highest standard. After a sensational performance, his eight-year-old co-star tells him:

  • TRUDI
  • That was the best acting I’ve ever seen in my entire life.

Back in 2019, I saw the film twice. On the second viewing, I watched the film's limited IMAX release with my mom. I was worried about the lecture I would hear after the destruction of the hippies.

The climax is a thing of beauty. It is an experience of Tarantino's switchboard where our emotions are channeled like lines of electricity. We switch between anxiety, fear, humor, and disgust, sometimes experiencing them all at once.

While Cliff is tripping on acid and trying to feed his dog, the hippies break in and enter Rick's home. We are anxious about the hippies' seriousness in murdering our favorite characters. Cliff laughs at Tex, a hippie pointing a gun at him. Cliff has met him, but can't remember his name.

  • TEX
  • I’m the Devil. And I’m here to do the Devil’s business.
  • CLIFF
  • …Nah, it was dumber than that. Something like Rex.
  • SADIE
  • God, shoot him, Tex!

And so, the battle begins. Cliff's dog mauls Tex. Then, Cliff throws a can of dog food straight into Sadie's face. Quite quickly, the hippies get mutilated. It should be disgusting, but the violence becomes funny. The crunch of bitten flesh, a knife impaling a hippie's thigh, a punch to the face, and a collapse to the floor. The hippies' screams become our satisfaction.

One hippie gets a knife into Cliff's hip. For that, Cliff slams her head into a telephone, a glass poster frame, a brick fireplace, and a coffee table. The first hits make us wince, but the repetition makes it comedic.

Tarantino lingers on the beatdown of these hippies as Cliff Booth and his dog punish them for trespassing with the intent to murder. When Rick brings out his flame thrower from his film, The Fourteen Fists of McCluskey, to torch the final hippie, it is the ultimate catharsis.

On paper, this entire sequence would appall my mom. Out of everyone in the theater, my mom was laughing the hardest. Why?

Through Tarantino's switchboard, we experience things in ways that we never expected. He converts an anxious situation into a funny one, disgusts us, and then makes us laugh again. Tarantino takes our anger at the murder of innocent people and delivers safe retribution on the screen. It all services an emotional truth: We wished his version of events was the real one.

At the heart of his film, there's something purer than torching murderous hippies. It is a classic buddy movie, about witnessing the bond between two friends. As the narrator says, to each other, they are "more than a brother and a little less than a wife."

When a medically stable Cliff leaves on ambulance, Rick runs up to the back glass to offer a parting word.

  • RICK
  • Hey, hey!
  • (whistles)
  • You're a good friend, Cliff.
  • CLIFF
  • (salutes)
  • I try.

A secure Rick places his hand on the glass and smiles, watching the ambulance drive down the hill of Cielo Drive. Even in the moonlight, it has same feeling of a cowboy riding off into the sunset, leaving behind a better world.
