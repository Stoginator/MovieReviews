Batman Begins

August 29, 2026 - 4:32 PM - Standard

A fallible superhero.

Why do we fall, sir?

Spoilers Ahead

By name, 2005's Batman Begins is a superhero film. By experience, it has more in common with film noir. The hero is not plainly a hero. He attacks Gotham's underbelly with a ferocity, driven by vengeance for his parent's murder and held back by his family name. His identity as Batman protects those he loves, but shields his life as Bruce Wayne from the consequences of the havoc he wreaks on Gotham's streets.

This is a film that leans hard into the pure origins of the comic-book character, capturing his traumatic childhood and showing how it bleeds into his adult life. His burning desire to fight injustice is such an obsession that Batman becomes Bruce Wayne's true identity. Does this sound like a summer blockbuster?

By the end, Rachel, Bruce Wayne's childhood friend, caresses his unmasked face.

  • RACHEL
  • This... is your mask. Your real face is the one that criminals now fear.

Writer-director Christopher Nolan is always looking for gaps in the marketplace. By partnering with an comic-book expert, David S. Goyer, they found that Bruce Wayne's origin story was never properly explored in the comics or on screen. It's a surprising discovery, one that can unlock a story. It fit neatly into the construct of Nolan's favorite superhero film, Richard Donner's Superman from 1978.

Donner's Superman traced the pivotal events of Clark Kent's childhood and how it leads him to the cape. Donner surrounded his lead, Christopher Reeve, with serious dramatic actors like Marlon Brando, Gene Hackman, and Glenn Ford. Nolan followed his example.

In Batman Begins, we spend time with Bruce as a boy, a college student, and an adult before he dons the cowl. The various figures in his life – Ra's al Ghul, Alfred, Gordon, Fox, and Mr. Earle – are played by Liam Neeson, Michael Caine, Gary Oldman, Morgan Freeman, and Rutger Hauer. Comic-book fans are happy, and casual viewers feel in on the joke, too. With them interacting with Bruce, we find ourselves drawn to the character itself, minimizing the importance of whether he is wearing the suit.

Nolan thematically connects Bruce Wayne as an adult to his journey through childhood. We find Bruce Wayne as an adult, facing off criminals in a prison in Bhutan. Using flashbacks and montages (which flow effortlessly), we see Bruce reckoning with the guilt he carries for causing his parents' death. When he attempts to murder his parents' killer, he learns he is broken inside and leaves Gotham to fix himself. At this point, we want Bruce to overcome his guilt.

  • BRUCE
  • As a man, I'm flesh and blood. I can be ignored. I can be destroyed. But as a symbol, I can be incorruptible, I can be everlasting.

So follows the Batman. Now that we understand Bruce Wayne, the Batman is no longer a myth to us. It is a fallible, mortal symbol, trying to stem the corruption of Gotham City. Nolan succeeds in making Batman and Gotham feel like real things.

When Batman is fighting on-screen, Nolan borrows from 1975's Jaws: "Less is more." We see a flurry of movement and a great deal of shadows. The pace of cutting jumps, the camera rotates, things are not perfectly capturing in-frame. We sense the chaos, the confusion, and the fear that Batman produces on his enemies. We understand that none of these fights are cakewalks; they're hard and the danger feels real.

Even more thought went into the philosophy of Batman Begins. Listen to these characters talk! Their conversations are battles of ideas. Here's one between Rachel and Bruce:

  • RACHEL
  • What chance does Gotham have when the good people do nothing?
  • BRUCE
  • I'm not one of your good people, Rachel.

And another between the film's villain and Bruce:

  • RA'S
  • Every time a civilization reaches the pinnacle of its decadence, we return to restore the balance. You were my greatest student. It should be you standing by my side, saving the world.
  • BRUCE
  • I'll be standing where I belong. Between you and the people of Gotham.
  • RA'S
  • No one can save Gotham.

It's rare for any film to sound this way.

Nolan's big-budget sensibilities were born on Batman Begins. They are neatly encapsulated by one scene, when Bruce climbs up a mountain to find the League of Shadows. Nolan integrates on-location set builds (by a Icelandic glacier), the film score, and loud, environmental sound effects to make the world extend off the screen. Even though his strength is failing him in the frigid temperatures with no food, Bruce will find a way up to learn how to combat injustice. The scene is fleeting, but it is character-driven and purely immersive.

Yet, for all the pomp and circumstance, the ending is a Nolan-signature: A blueprint of reality stamped onto a fictional story. Rarely is real-life purely triumphant; more often, present success and foreboding go together. At the close, Batman has won his first battle, but another one is coming. Bruce Wayne's vulnerability will follow him there.
