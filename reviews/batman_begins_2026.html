Tanish's Movie Reviews

Batman Begins

August 29, 2026 - 4:32 PM - Standard

A fallible superhero.

Why do we fall, sir?

Spoilers Ahead

By name, 2005's Batman Begins is a superhero film. By experience, it has more in common with film noir. The protagonist is not plainly a hero. He ferociously attacks Gotham's underbelly, driven by vengeance for his parent's murder. His anger is kept at bay by his family name. Batman's double identity protects those he loves, but shields Bruce Wayne from the consequences of the havoc he wreaks on Gotham's streets. Not the typical altruism of Spidey or Superman, is it?

This is a film that leans hard into the pure origins of the comic-book character, capturing his traumatic childhood and showing how it bleeds into his adult life. His burning desire to fight injustice is such an obsession that Batman becomes Bruce Wayne's true identity. If it wasn't called Batman, would it sound like a summer blockbuster?

Writer-director Christopher Nolan is always looking for gaps in the marketplace. By partnering with an comic-book expert, David S. Goyer, they found that Bruce Wayne's origin story was never properly explored in the comics or on screen. It's a surprising discovery, one that can unlock a story. It fit neatly into the construct of Nolan's favorite superhero film, Richard Donner's Superman from 1978.

Donner's Superman traced the pivotal events of Clark Kent's childhood and how it leads him to the cape. Donner surrounded his lead, Christopher Reeve, with serious dramatic actors like Marlon Brando, Gene Hackman, and Glenn Ford. Nolan followed his example.

Unusually, Nolan cast Christian Bale in the role before the script was even writing, going against his philosophy of casting against a finished script. With Bale, Nolan saw an obsessive quality that could draw the audience in. What kind of actor can lose 60 pounds for one role (The Machinist) and gain a hundred more to play Batman? Only someone who truly believes in the craft of acting like Bruce Wayne believes in justice.

The various figures in Bruce's life – Ra's al Ghul, Alfred, Gordon, Fox, and Mr. Earle – are played by Liam Neeson, Michael Caine, Gary Oldman, Morgan Freeman, and Rutger Hauer. Comic-book fans are happy, and casual viewers feel in on the joke, too. With them interacting with Bruce, we find ourselves drawn to the character itself, minimizing the importance of whether he is wearing the suit.

Using an inability to process guilt as a thematic connection, Nolan weaves Bruce Wayne's story together from childhood to present day. First, we find Bruce Wayne as an adult, facing off criminals in a prison in Bhutan. Using flashbacks and montages (which flow effortlessly), we see Bruce as a child, reckoning with the idea that he caused his parents' death. As a college student, he attempts to murder his parents' killer himself. When a childhood friend, Rachel, scolds him for this, he learns he is broken inside. He leaves Gotham to fix himself. At this point, we want Bruce to overcome his guilt.

  • BRUCE
  • As a man, I'm flesh and blood. I can be ignored. I can be destroyed. But as a symbol, I can be incorruptible, I can be everlasting.

So follows the Batman. Now that we understand Bruce Wayne, the Batman is no longer a myth to us. It is a fallible, mortal symbol, trying to stem the corruption of Gotham City. Nolan succeeds in making Batman and Gotham feel like real things.

When Batman is fighting on-screen, Nolan borrows from 1975's Jaws: "Less is more." We see a flurry of movement and a great deal of shadows. The pace of cutting jumps, the camera rotates around the action, things are not perfectly captured in-frame. We sense the chaos, the confusion, and the fear that Batman produces on his enemies. We understand that none of these fights are cakewalks; the danger feels real.

The best example is Bruce Wayne's sword fight with Ra's al Ghul on ice. It begins as a training session, trying to avoid falling into the freezing water underneath. It introduces us to the idea that fighting in Batman Begins has risk.

Ra's al Ghul makes the session personal:

  • RA'S
  • Your parents' death was not your fault.... It was your father's.

Bruce's next blows are filled with fury. In response, Ra's al Ghul says:

  • RA'S
  • Anger does not change the fact that your father failed to act.

There is a dramatic weight placed on the action. It escalates. Our eyes don't glaze over.

Even more thought went into the philosophy of Batman Begins. Listen to these characters talk! Their conversations are battles of ideas. Here's one between Bruce and Alfred:

  • BRUCE
  • Should I just bury the past out there with my parents, Alfred?
  • Alfred
  • I wouldn't presume to tell you what to do with your past, sir. Just know that there are those of us who care about what you do with your future.

And another between Ra's al Ghul and Bruce:

  • RA'S
  • Every time a civilization reaches the pinnacle of its decadence, we return to restore the balance.... You were my greatest student. It should be you standing by my side, saving the world.
  • BRUCE
  • I'll be standing where I belong. Between you and the people of Gotham.
  • RA'S
  • No one can save Gotham.

By the end, Rachel, Bruce Wayne's childhood friend, caresses his unmasked face.

  • RACHEL
  • This... is your mask. Your real face is the one that criminals now fear.

It's rare for a film to sound this way – and still make room for the quippy, crowd-pleasing zingers.

  • ALFRED
  • You can borrow the Rolls, if you like. Just bring it back with a full tank.

Nolan's big-budget sensibilities were born on Batman Begins. They are neatly encapsulated by one scene, when Bruce climbs up a mountain to find the League of Shadows. Nolan integrates on-location set builds (by a Icelandic glacier), the film score, and loud, environmental sound effects to make the world extend off the screen. Even though Bruce's strength is failing, in the frigid temperatures with no food, he is determined to summit and learn how to combat injustice. The scene is fleeting, but it is character-driven and purely immersive.

Yet, for all the pomp and circumstance, the ending is a Nolan-signature: A blueprint of reality stamped onto a fictional story. Rarely is real-life purely triumphant; more often, present success and foreboding go together. Superhero films are almost never coated in ambivalence. The hero always wins.

At the close of Batman Begins, Batman has won his first battle against Ra's al Ghul, but another one is coming. For every principle that the League of Shadows claimed to have, the next villain's main tool is anarchy. The Joker has no code. Those encounters will be laden with risk. And all of Bruce Wayne's vulnerabilities will follow him there: The moral boundaries, the double identity, and especially the guilt.
